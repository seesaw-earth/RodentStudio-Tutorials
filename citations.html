Three rules for citing data

  1. Cite the dataset itself, by its DOI, in the reference list, and also the paper its authors ask you to cite. A dataset and its paper are separate works.
  2. Cite software with its version. “Ultralytics YOLO26”, “RodentStudio 0.7.1”, “coremltools 9.0”: results can change between versions.
  3. Say what you changed and where labels came from. CC BY 4.0 requires credit, a link to the licence and an indication of changes. Here the labels were harmonized and filtered from DeepLabCut and JAX-network predictions; write that, rather than implying human annotation.

If you used the TST detector

Cite RodentStudio [S1] and Ultralytics YOLO11 [S3], and report whether the YOLO or the classical detector was used (RodentStudio's exports record this). For RodentFlow workflows, no model citation is needed; describe the classical tracker and cite RodentStudio.

If you cite the checks in these tutorials

  • CalMS21 zero-shot test: the CalMS21 dataset [D5] and paper [P9]; the MARS keypoints come from MARS [P10].
  • Elevated-plus-maze pilot: Sturman et al. 2020 [P11] and the video record [D6]; DLCAnalyzer [S5] if you used its scripts.
  • PhysioNet demonstration video (cylinder v1): the dataset [D7] and, as PhysioNet asks, the PhysioNet platform paper [P12].
  • Exporting to Core ML: coremltools [S4].

Example methods paragraph

Adapt this to what you actually did; replace everything in square brackets.

Pose estimation. Six body keypoints (nose, left ear, right ear, neck, body centre and tail base) were estimated in every frame with the MousePose6 model (MousePose6_960_int8, a YOLO26n-pose network [S2] with 8-bit weights) run on-device through Core ML in RodentStudio [version] [S1]. The model was trained on 6,140 frames from 36 videos of four public top-view datasets [D1–D4], using as labels the DeepLabCut [P4, P5] and JAX pose-network [P3] predictions released with those datasets after harmonizing them to a common keypoint set and filtering them for quality; the labels are therefore model-derived rather than human annotations. On held-out videos from the same datasets it detected 94.6 % of mice, and 96.1 % of the keypoints of detected mice lay within 0.1 body lengths of the reference labels. We checked keypoint placement on [number] randomly chosen frames per video from our own recordings [and compared the derived measures with blinded manual scoring of [number] videos].
